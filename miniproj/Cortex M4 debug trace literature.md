# Mapping thin Cortex-M4 debug-trace literature

**The literature on Cortex-M4 (ARMv7-M) debug and trace for real-time use is small, scattered and mostly indirect: about 20 peer-reviewed papers touch the topic, but only a handful make Cortex-M4 debug or trace hardware their subject.** The core is FrankenTrace (cycle-level tracing through DWT, ITM and SWO), muAFL (ETM trace filtered by DWT comparators) and the 2023 timing papers that use the DWT cycle counter as ground truth. Most other work uses the hardware as a tool, for fuzzing, shadow stacks or hotpatching, and reports real-time cost as an incidental overhead figure. Three kinds of material fill the gaps: Arm manuals, vendor and practitioner notes (Memfault, Lauterbach, SEGGER, Percepio) and Cortex-A trace research that does not transfer cleanly to the M4. Several topics a real-time engineer would expect to be covered have no verified paper: DebugMonitor debugging, SWO/ITM bandwidth and probe effect, HardFault/CFSR diagnosis, SystemView or Tracealyzer overhead, and DWT/ETM-based WCET on Cortex-M. Absence from this search is not proof of absence in the literature: IEEE Xplore and ACM DL full-text searches were not reachable, and a database search by a person with access is the recommended next step. Every entry below is tagged by evidence tier, and nothing unverified is promoted.

## How to read the tiers

The notes support five tiers, and entries keep their tier throughout. **Peer-reviewed** means a venue and year were stated on a fetched publisher, proceedings, repository or arXiv-linked page. **Preprint** means arXiv-style material with no confirmed venue, so it is not treated as peer reviewed even where a venue is suspected. **Thesis or book** covers theses, which are not peer reviewed, and the three books. **Vendor/practitioner** covers manuals, blogs and vendor papers. **Unverified** means seen only as a title or listing, or with venue and authors unconfirmed. Three bibliographic discrepancies are flagged inline: author order for FrankenTrace and muAFL differs between sources, and the Beckmann and Steffan listing shows 2022 and 2023.

## Papers that use Cortex-M4 debug and trace hardware directly

This is the most relevant cluster, and it is short. The anchor is **FrankenTrace**, which shows that cycle-level PC and data-transfer traces are obtainable on ARMv7-M parts without ETM, using only DWT, ITM, SWO and a cheap probe, with adjustable invasiveness ([UW repository](https://repozytorium.uw.edu.pl/entities/publication/231c85db-3999-4073-8e27-95120e7c243e/full)). It defines what the low-bandwidth path can and cannot deliver, and the notes flag that the full PDF still needs reading for the DWT details. The other end of the split is **muAFL**, which streams ETM instruction trace and uses DWT comparators to switch tracing on and off for code regions or tasks, so the target keeps its timing ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.03013)). That split matters in practice because ETM is optional and absent on many M4 parts; the notes cite the STM32F446 as an example, an inference rather than a sourced fact.

Two lessons for real-time work come out of this cluster. WATSON is the only fetched paper to exercise DebugMonitor as an actual trap, and it never measures what an exception entry costs against a deadline, so the real-time price of DWT-triggered monitors is an open question the notes treat as unanswered. And the notes' inference holds up: low-speed tracing (DWT/ITM/SWO) and high-speed ETM are the practical fork on M4 silicon, and FrankenTrace and muAFL sit on opposite sides of it.

## Timing, WCET and RTOS measurement show how the M4 is instrumented in practice

No verified paper makes DWT, ETM, ITM or SWO measurement-based WCET on Cortex-M its core contribution. What exists is two 2023 preprints that use the cycle counter as ground truth, plus RTOS timing studies that mostly use GPIO and an oscilloscope. El Yaacoub et al. read the DWT cycle counter before and after each code section on an STM32L432KC (Cortex-M4F), 500 samples per benchmark ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.14213)). Arnström et al. report a measured WCET of 136,809 cycles against an aiT static bound about 40 times higher, with the prefetch cache flushed before each solve ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.11576)); the notes warn that the page's "84 GHz" is almost certainly a typo for 84 MHz, and the timer is described as "onboard", with DWT not named.

## Trace bandwidth, compression and replay research is solid but not Cortex-M

The trace-port bottleneck has good academic treatment from outside the M4 world. Uzelac, Milenkovic, Burtscher and Milenkovic (CASES 2010) compress execution traces in real time using branch-predictor events, reaching about **0.036 bits per instruction**, roughly 28 times below commercial trace modules, in about 5,200 gates ([PDF](https://userweb.cs.txstate.edu/~mb92/papers/cases10.pdf)). The IEEE Transactions on Computers extension reports about 0.029 bits per instruction ([PDF](https://userweb.cs.txstate.edu/~mb92/papers/tc14.pdf)); its final year is unconfirmed (the file name suggests 2014). Neither compares against ETMv3 on an M4 numerically. On the decoding side, Zeinolabedin, Partzsch and Mayr (DATE 2021, pp. 1606-1609) show ETMv4 data trace is normally decoded in software for only a few seconds of data, and propose a 125 MHz FPGA decoder ([DATE](https://past.date-conference.com/proceedings-archive/2021/pdf/1005.pdf)). ETMv4 is not the ETMv3 of the Cortex-M4, so treat the result as indirect.

For halting versus replay, the Mälardalen group's work argues that breakpoints and single-stepping alter real-time timing and proposes record-then-replay. Sundmark et al. applied replay debugging to an ABB robot controller and a SAAB radar system (AADEBUG 2003, [arXiv cs/0311019](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0311019)). It predates Cortex-M and uses RTOS-level logging, not CoreSight, so it is motivation, not evidence for M4.

## Gaps: topics where no verified paper was found

The researchers found the following holes, and they are the practical finding of this review. Each should be read as "not found in this search", not "does not exist".

DebugMonitor and monitor-mode debugging has no dedicated academic paper; the only substantive treatment is the Memfault blog plus WATSON and Watchmen using it as a trap. CoreSight on Cortex-M has no survey, in contrast to Cortex-A. SWO and ITM bandwidth limits, and the probe effect of printf-style ITM tracing on M4 timing, have no verified quantitative study beyond Beckmann and Steffan's handling of lossy SWO, and no verified study covers on-chip trace buffers on the M4 itself. No paper uses DWT event counters (CPI, LSU, fold, sleep) for anomaly or intrusion detection beyond Biswas et al.'s control-flow checks, and no ETM-based control-flow integrity for Cortex-M turned up.

For timing, there is no verified paper on DWT/ETM/ITM/SWO-based measurement-based or hybrid WCET specifically on Cortex-M, and none measuring M4 interrupt latency with DWT, ITM or SWO. Record-and-replay or reverse debugging on Cortex-M hardware trace produced only patents. No verified peer-reviewed study covers SEGGER SystemView, Tracealyzer overhead or probe effect on Cortex-M, nor ChibiOS tracing, nor an LTTng-style embedded tracer for Cortex-M, nor a SystemView-versus-ETM overhead comparison. For fault handling, no paper was found primarily about HardFault/CFSR/HFSR/MMFAR/BFAR diagnosis, post-mortem analysis, or RTOS stack-overflow detection on Cortex-M, and none on M4-specific debug lockout (STM32F4 RDP, nRF52 APPROTECT) beyond blogs. HERA, the FPB-based hotpatcher, was not located as a paper. Not searched in depth: WCET workshop proceedings (OASIcs 2017-2024), EMSOFT, LCTES and RTAS tracks, and IEEE Xplore and ACM DL full text, so a database search under these keywords (ITM, SWO, DWT, STM32, CoreSight with tracing or WCET) is the obvious next step.

## Conclusion

The practical reading order for a real-time Cortex-M4 project is: Arm DDI 0403 and Lauterbach for the hardware model, FrankenTrace and muAFL for what trace can deliver at low and high bandwidth, the Memfault note for DebugMonitor, then Ungurean, Miranda and El Yaacoub for how timing is actually measured today. Beckmann and Steffan and WATSON round out the picture of what monitors cost.

The more useful finding is negative and consistent across all three note sets: nobody has published the real-time cost of the M4's own debug hardware. Exception-entry cost of a DWT-triggered DebugMonitor trap, SWO and ITM loss under load, and SystemView or Tracealyzer overhead against hardware trace all lack verified numbers, while Cortex-A work (Vergé, ARMOR, the ETMv4 decoder) is the only quantitative comparison available and cannot be assumed to transfer. A project that measures any of these with DWT CYCCNT on a real M4 would be filling a documented gap, and the notes explicitly flag the fault-detection and SystemView-on-FreeRTOS corner as open for positioning.
